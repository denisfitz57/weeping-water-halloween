Goblins and Popcorn Balls

Eighty-five years of Halloween in Weeping Water, from soaped windows and buggies on the roof to costume parades, Trick or Treat for UNICEF, and a haunted house south of town called Dread Haven.

Weeping Water · Avoca · Nehawka · Elmwood · Murdock1901 to 1998884 pages that mention Halloween

Halloween came late to the Weeping Water Republican. The paper was founded in 1882, but the word does not appear in any surviving page until 1901. After that it turns up nearly every autumn: a sentence or two at first, then whole columns of parties, school news, pranks, prizes and advertisements.

Read straight through, those columns tell a story. In the early years Halloween belonged to the town's boys and young men, and the paper spent each November counting the damage. By the 1920s and 1930s, churches, lodges, schools and dance halls were filling the night with parties, partly to keep the pranksters busy. In 1955 the town's churches turned trick-or-treating into a door-to-door collection for UNICEF. And through all of it, children kept showing up in costumes that say a lot about their times, from ghosts and Gypsies to Harry Truman, a Rubik's Cube and a member of Kiss.

1907–1951The night before

Soap, Buggies and the Morning After

For half a century, the paper's Halloween coverage was mostly about what the boys had done.

In 1907 the Republican warned that “there may be something doing in town, for that is Hallowe’en,” and offered the boys a rule of thumb: before wrecking anything, ask “How would my own parents like to be served that way?” The editor wasn't against fun. He just wanted the kind “that cost nothing to replace.”

The pranks that followed were mostly about moving things. In 1912 someone unhitched the teams outside a party at Henry Snell's farm, scattered the buggies, and hid the Marshall boys' team across town, where it turned up tied to a tree near Hans Johnson's home. The “would-be-jokers,” the paper admitted, had at least “showed sense enough and heart enough to blanket the team.” In 1915 James P. Johnson ran a notice asking whoever took his buggy to return the lap robe and whip they had kept. In 1913, at Berlin, the boys hardly bothered because a tornado had already done the upsetting, though nobody could say whether Henry Junge's loose hogs were a prank or an open gate.

By 1917 the town put extra police on duty for Halloween night, and “a number of traces of the mischievious boys” were still visible in the morning. In 1920 the pranks stretched over a whole week. In 1921 they went far enough that some of the older boys were hauled before a justice and given until Wednesday morning to repair the damage at Mr. Pickrell's or go to jail. That same year someone opened a hydrant at Bert Reed's corner, which, the editor noted, “would have been extremely funny if we had had a fire.” The paper's printer then made it worse: the story said the pranks had gone beyond the ludicrous to the “comical,” and the next week the editor had to explain that the word should have been “criminal.”

Most years were milder. The soaped windows of 1927 and the tires and boxes stacked on the Switzer Brothers office roof drew only a shrug: “boys will be boys, and we’re all strong for the boys, and girls.” By 1949 “watchful eyes of the police” kept the damage downtown to a minimum, and by 1951 the paper could look back on the old days, when people woke to find a buggy on top of the town hall or someone else's livestock in the basement, and say “we don’t know what Halloween is all about—thank goodness.”

“We wouldn’t give two cents for a boy who couldn’t find enjoyment in 4th of July, Hallowe’en or Christmas time.”Weeping Water Republican, October 31, 1907

The best prank story arrived in 1979, as a reader's memory from another town. The local boys tipped the same farmer's outhouse every year, until one Halloween he moved it a few feet off the hole.

1912–1955A local tradition

Follow the Rope to the Cellar

The same party game shows up for more than forty years: a dark house, a rope or string, and a fortune at the end.

Local tradition

Again and again, hosts led their guests through a dark house by a string or rope, past ghosts and spooky figures, down to the cellar, where a ghost or a Gypsy fortune-teller waited. The Republican described it in 1912, 1921 and 1955, and the high school used a blindfolded version in 1926.

At the Heebner home in 1912 the guests were met at the door by young Granville Heebner “dressed as a ghost,” given little booklets of directions, and sent on “one of the most ghostly trips”: following a string to the cellar, where a ghost handed each of them a fortune, later burned.

When Gladys Philpot invited forty friends in 1921, an old lady with “a cold clammy hand” met them at the door and insisted on shaking hands. With no lights on, the guests followed a rope through the house to a clown, then two spooky figures upstairs, then down to the cellar, where Evelin Doty, dressed as a Gypsy, told their fortunes. Afterward they bobbed for apples, played the Edison phonograph, and the boys shelled corn while the girls popped it.

In 1926 the whole high school was led blindfolded through the basement and up and down stairs “and in the wandering met with various ghostly articles.” By 1955 the game had grown teeth. At the Schmidt home a realistic ghost (Mrs. Betts) sent guests along a rope around the house, where an old witch (Mrs. Schmidt) made them crawl through a dark room over sheets hiding wet sponges, egg shells, wet noodles and balloons. Arthur Schmidt won best dressed man that night, “though he was dressed as a woman.”

1913–1983For the grown-ups

Hard Times, Masquerades and the Chamber of Horrors

The town's adults had their own Halloween, at the opera house, the dance palace, the auditorium and the Legion Club.

Local tradition

“Hard Times” costumes. A Hard Times party meant dressing as poorly as you could, in patched overalls and old clothes. The Republican reports a Hard Times Halloween party at the Dowler country home in 1917, high-schoolers dressed “in hard time style” in 1926 so nobody would spoil a good suit, a “Hard Time” Halloween party for the JCC in 1955, and a Hard-Time Costume Dance and Masquerade at the Agricultural Auditorium in 1956.

The Halloween dance at Philpot's opera house in 1913 promised to be “the biggest one of the season.” In 1927 the high school alumni took over Philpot Hall for a full carnival, with goblins, ghosts, fortune tellers, fish ponds, vaudeville, horns, whistles and confetti, and a “Chamber of Horrors” promising “a whole set of new thrills.” In 1931 Blondy Baughan's orchestra played a carnival Halloween dance at the Dowler Dance Palace, with free doughnuts and coffee. In 1940 King's orchestra came from Omaha for a masquerade at the auditorium, for 20 and 30 cents.

Costume contests were part of the fun, and sometimes part of the joke. At the American Legion Halloween dance in 1944, David and Harold Myers took the $10 prize for best costumes by “fooling the judges into believing they were a boy and girl.” The story was funny enough that the paper reprinted it in its “40 Years Ago” column in 1984.

The Legion Club kept the masquerade going in the 1960s, the Elms Roller Rink offered a prize for best costume on skates in 1956, and in 1983 Wanda's Last Chance Saloon advertised a Halloween party and dance with “prizes for best costume.”

1917–1967School notes

Popcorn Balls and the Parade Through the Grades

The children's own school columns describe Halloween from the inside.

Local tradition

The costume parade through the school. On Halloween the younger grades put on costumes and paraded through the other classrooms. Pupils describe it in 1955 (“We paraded through the high school rooms and most of the grades”), 1966 and 1967 (“We enjoyed the parade of all the lower grades celebrating Halloween in costume”).

In 1917 Miss Marshall's fourth grade drew witches for the classroom, and only the best handwriting went up on display. In 1930 the kindergartners, finally getting their news into print, gave a party for their mothers: a winking jack-o-lantern at the door, a hunt for tiny hidden jack-o-lanterns (Dick Power found the most), a pantomime in leaf costumes, a “big north wind” played by John Towle, and Bernice Keech in “a very clever costume” scattering snow over everyone.

Pupils made pumpkin men with hats and neckties in 1931 and held a funniest-mask contest in 1933, followed by pumpkin pie and whipped cream in a nearby grove. In 1952 the refreshment committee went to Mrs. Ketelhut's house to make popcorn balls for a party with Kool Aid and ice cream bars, and Penny Sue Van Every's mother painted a large witch for the room.

1912–1992Who came as what

Mr. and Mrs. Harry Truman, a Dalmatian and a Member of Kiss

The paper loved to list who won the costume prize, and the lists read like a history of popular culture.

  • 1912 Granville Heebner, a ghost, greeting guests at the door
  • 1921 Evelin Doty, a Gypsy fortune-teller in the cellar
  • 1930 Bernice Keech, the snow, in a kindergarten pantomime
  • 1934 Donajean, best costume at the Avoca Willing Workers club
  • 1940 William Wheatley, a ghost who kept his identity secret the longest
  • 1944 David and Harold Myers, “a boy and girl” ($10 prize)
  • 1951 Ramona and Maria Gardner, as Mr. and Mrs. Harry Truman
  • 1955 Jolene Spohn, the “Yellow Rose of Texas”; Mary Nichols, the Cavalier; and Ma and Pa Kettle
  • 1956 Sandra Baker's fifth birthday: three witches, three ghosts, a Spanish dancer, Annie Oakley, a rat, a clown, two cowboys and a ballerina
  • 1959 Brownies Ramona Severn, a Miss America contestant, and Joan Domingo, a clown
  • 1976 Avoca: Queen of Hearts, Frankenstein, a witch, a cowboy and Raggedy Ann
  • 1983 Brownies and Girl Scouts: a Rubik's Cube, a werewolf, the Queen of Hearts, a Gypsy, and a mother dressed as a member of Kiss
  • 1985 Murdock Cub Scouts, all pirates
  • 1992 Alex Hibler, kindergartner, the Dalmatian

Ghosts, witches and Gypsies run through the whole century. Cowboys and Annie Oakley belong to the 1950s, when television westerns ruled. The 1980s brought the Rubik's Cube and rock bands, and mothers increasingly joined in.

1955–1965A new wrinkle

Trick or Treat for UNICEF

In 1955 Weeping Water's churches tried something new: send the goblins out with milk cartons.

Local tradition

For at least a decade, Weeping Water children trick-or-treated for coins instead of candy. They met at the churches or the Agricultural Auditorium at 6:30, went out in groups with an adult sponsor, carried milk cartons with orange and black UNICEF stickers, and came back for a party with refreshments. The Chamber of Commerce supplied the pop.

“A new wrinkle will be added to the time honored ‘Trick or Treat’ program by Weeping Water children this Halloween,” the paper announced in October 1955. Instead of gathering “loot, such as cookies, candy, fruit, etc., by the bagful,” children would collect cash for the United Nations Children's Fund, “the first time it has been tried in Weeping Water.” On Halloween night the church bells rang at 6:30 to send out spooks in orange tags, each asking residents “to help him share his Halloween with all the children of the world.”

In 1957 about 115 “spooks, goblins, or what-have-you” collected $106, and the paper explained that a nickel bought 80 glasses of milk. In 1959 Mrs. Neil Fitzpatrick answered doubters with her six-year-old's words: “Mom, it’s nice to help little orphans and sick babies, isn’t it?” By 1962 the children were organized by grade at three churches, and adults were warned that a flashlight and “plenty of endurance” would help them keep up with the spooks. In Elmwood in 1964 the UNICEF committee went further and asked residents to give treats only to UNICEF collectors, to “discourage the house to house ‘Candy Tricks or Treats.’”

1923–1976Main Street

Painted Windows and the Avoca Parade

The merchants learned that the best way to protect their windows was to give the children something to do with them.

In 1923 J. J. Meier's drug store window, with hobgoblins, pumpkin lanterns and “a profusion of autumn leaves,” was pretty enough to earn its own story. Fifty years later the windows belonged to the children. In 1975 the Weeping Water Chamber of Commerce “once again” sponsored a window painting contest with cash prizes in two age groups, while Chamber members “once more” patrolled the downtown streets on Halloween night.

Avoca's merchants went all in. In 1973 they sponsored a Halloween costume parade and party on Main Street, with ten third prizes, treats for every child and doughnuts and coffee for the adults. In 1974 they added a pancake feed and a free dance with Ernie Emshoff's orchestra, and in 1976 the Lions Club took over.

1934–1982Advertisements

Candy by the Pound, Ham and a Midnight Show

Halloween in the advertising columns.

Local stores and theaters used Halloween to sell. In 1934 the Liberty Theatre suggested “a Hallowe’en Theatre Party” at the bargain price of 15 cents for Murder in the Private Car. In 1939 a grocery was “proud of our candy dept.”, with Halloween chocolates, creams, nougats and caramels at 20 cents a pound and candy bars three for a dime. In 1940 another grocery invited children to stop by between 7 and 9 on Halloween night for “a sure prize,” in the middle of an ad for Swift's Premium Ham. The Wef Theatre ran a Halloween midnight show in 1941, R. O. Baker offered Indian corn for decorations with his roasting chickens in 1960, and by 1982 the ads were for M&M's and Brach's candy corn by the 16-ounce bag.

1972–1993Spook houses

The Invaders and Dread Haven

Trick-or-treating, as reported by a newspaper with a sense of humor, and the haunted houses that followed.

On November 1, 1973, the Republican reported the year's trick-or-treating as an alien invasion. A horde of goblins, ghosts and witches struck at 6:30 p.m., stunning housewives with “horrible appearing monstrosities” shouting “Trick or Treat” in childish trebles. The invaders withdrew after 9:00 when the candy ran out. Experts, the paper said, believed they came from another planet and lived on candy for a year, so Weeping Water should be safe until October 31, 1974. One invader, questioned at the door, broke cover: “I’m a witch, but my name is Angie Wright.”

Haunted houses became the new tradition. The Youth Center's Spook House entertained 130 people in 1972, run by grade school and junior high students under Peggy Simons, with a promise of “a bigger and better Spook House” next year. In 1992 the Congregational Church School's “Mystery Manor” drew more than 900 people, and in 1993, for a second year on Lyle and Jean Thomassen's land south of town, the church presented “Dread Haven,” five rooms with guides, a concession stand, and a very large troll named Griffin. It was “a good clean theatrical scare for the entire family, well maybe not the tiny tots,” and was pointedly “not advertised in Omaha or Lincoln.” The same week Elmwood's FBLA turned the school into a haunted house and charged a can of food for the pantry.

1991–1998Later years

The Trick of the Century

An ice storm postpones Halloween, and the newspaper office becomes a trick-or-treat stop.

On Halloween 1991, freezing rain coated Weeping Water in ice. Schools closed, power failed, and city officials postponed trick-or-treating until Sunday. The Journal called it “the Halloween trick of the century,” with “Mother Nature the obvious prankster.” By the next morning the town was in a blizzard. Rathe's Variety sold every flashlight battery but four packages, Meeske Ace sold out of kerosene heaters and lamps, and people shopped at Hartman's Family Foods by flashlight while Bernie's Cafe, the Town Tap and the Dairy Sweet kept serving on their gas stoves.

Later in the decade, Halloween settled into a daytime ritual for the youngest children. Head Start pupils and the Gooseberry Gardens day care toured downtown in costume in 1996, collecting candy at the Republican office, and Murdock's preschoolers paraded to the bank, the post office and the Bull Dogs Bar and Grill for treats in 1998 before carving pumpkins.

1943–1975Handle with care

When Halloween Went Wrong

A few clippings describe fires, serious vandalism or worries about harm to children. They're covered by a content note; click one to show it.

In more than 800 pages we found no deaths and no fireworks injuries connected with Halloween in the Weeping Water area. The worst it describes is a jack-o-lantern that set curtains alight in 1943, a night in Avoca in 1957 when vandals broke windows and started a fire at the oil station, and, from the early 1970s, the national scare about tampered treats that made parents check their children's candy bags. Safety advice became a yearly feature: fire-resistant costumes, masks you can see out of, and packaged candy instead of homemade treats.

Sources & Method

This history was built from the full-text OCR of 42,861 pages of the Weeping Water Republican (1886–1985), searching every October and November issue with error-tolerant patterns. The scans are often badly read, so the search allowed up to two wrong letters in key words (“Hallowe’en,” “jack-o-lantern,” “trick or treat,” “costume,” “goblin”) and caught forms like “Hal­lowe’en,” “Hauoween” and “jack-o-1antern” while excluding look-alikes such as the surname Hallowell. That found 884 pages. The Plattsmouth Journal (1986–2000), which absorbed the Republican, and missing pages were searched through the library's online archive, which also confirmed that Halloween does not appear in the paper before 1901.

Every clipping here was then read from the page image and transcribed by hand, because the OCR often runs neighboring columns together. Crops were placed using the page's text layer and checked by eye. The images were cleaned to flatten uneven lighting, reduce noise and restore a newsprint tone. Photographs from the 1980s survive only as harsh black-and-white scans; they were softened but not repainted.
